2021–2022 / an opening into the movement

What if a song
could be a
meeting place?

Musicians began releasing work as NFTs: distinct digital tokens that could be collected and transferred. The recording could still be heard and copied. What changed was the way a supporter could collect an edition—and the experiments artists could build around that relationship.

Some sold a single recording. Others funded a project, gathered a community or tried making music together. These were different answers to a shared problem: how to keep creating, with more say over the work and who it reached.

A record could circulate.
Its edition could have a history.

LATASHÁ / music as a visual practice

She had already
made the work.

In Minted, LATASHÁ describes label meetings that tried to make her fit a narrower idea of a rapper. She chose independence, making videos with the resources she had.

Then she minted an unreleased video, ilikedat. In her telling, an early bid mattered because it could pay rent. A music video could be encountered as a work of art, supported by someone who wanted it to exist.

Songcamp / 2021

The internet
became a room.

Collecting was only one kind of participation.
People also showed up to listen, trade ideas
and find someone to make something with.

From finding a scene
to making a band.

Songcamp organized around making work together. With CHAOS, that idea grew into rotating groups of musicians, alongside people building the art, software and structures around the release.

Beyond the release

A sale is a moment.
A scene is what
people keep doing.

LATASHÁ’s account moves from collecting to teaching. Songcamp’s retrospective follows the band into further experiments. The history becomes richer when we ask what the work made possible—and what happened when the conditions changed.

This design study follows a few connected scenes. The next research pass asks who else built these spaces, who was left out, and which promises held up.
