The photograph
A selected history / earlier experiments to afterlives

The music was everywhere.
The people making it wanted more say.

A play count could tell an artist how far a song travelled. It could not, by itself, create a lasting relationship with the people listening. Artists began testing another possibility: release work on their own terms, recognize its supporters and invite them into what happened next.

Non-fungible
Not interchangeable like-for-like. This particular item has its own identity and history—like a particular signed record.
Token
Something that represents something else: an object, an idea, even a feeling—as in a token of appreciation. Here, a digital record associated with a song or other work.

The music can still be copied and heard. The blockchain records the token’s ownership, not exclusive possession of the sound. The story is what people made possible around it.

The question / Benjamin James’s 2022 recollection

Who gets to decide what happens next?

Benjamin James knew what it meant to have someone else decide a record’s future. Writing as musicben in February 2022, he recalled being signed and dropped by a major label in 2014. Music NFTs interested him as another way to reach supporters and keep making work.

His question runs through this history: who gets to decide what happens around a song? To understand the experiments he was watching, we need to go back—before collectible records, to an artist trying to change the machinery behind a release.

Notes & connections

2015 / Imogen Heap, Mycelia & Ujo

A song should carry its people with it.

Years before the collecting boom, Imogen Heap was looking at the people a song’s title leaves out. Through Mycelia, she imagined a recording carrying its credits, permissions and connections with it. Tiny Human gave that idea a concrete test. In 2015, Heap and Ujo used Ethereum to connect licensing choices with automatic payments to contributors.

The prototype made part of the machinery behind a release visible and programmable. Ujo closed it in 2016 because maintaining it took resources from its next platform. That tension would return: an artist could imagine a different system, but somebody still had to build it, explain it and keep it working.

Notes & connections

May 2020 / RAC & Zora

Before the boom, a cassette.

In May 2020, RAC and Zora put an old-fashioned object at the center of a new release: 100 BOY cassettes. A digital token could be redeemed for a tape. Zora co-founder Jacob Horne traced the idea through a fashion experiment, conversations online and a group call.

The musician and the builders were designing the release together. These were fungible tokens, rather than unique NFT editions. But the experiment opened a question that would animate the next wave: what else could artists change if they could shape the tools around their work?

Notes & connections

March 2021 / Catalog

A record shop for the internet.

Jeremy Stern and Mike McKain approached that question as musicians and record diggers. Before Catalog, they built Loft Radio, where listeners could tip artists. Catalog, launched in March 2021, gave direct support a different form: a one-of-one digital record.

The music could stay available to hear while someone collected its edition. It was a proposition about attention as much as technology. A song could be treated as a particular work worth seeking out and supporting, with a visible history around it.

2021 / listening & collecting

Hear the song. Follow the record.

Listen to EPROM’s How U Make Me Feel. You do not need to own its NFT to hear it. The token connects this particular edition to a history of ownership. Copying the audio does not copy that history, and buying the token does not automatically buy the copyright.

Catalog’s founders pointed to another possibility: this record could be found through Catalog and bid on through Zora. Different front doors could lead to the same edition. But a record still needed people to find it.

2021–2023 / making a place to return

The room was a link.

Songcamp began in the disruption of the pandemic, when canceled shows and online isolation had changed how musicians could meet. Its first camp started in April 2021: nine musicians, two visual artists and two operators making songs and a release together. Twitter led people into Discord; calls became a working space.

The virtual gathering pictured here belongs to that early world. A collectible might offer a new way to release music, but the camp was asking an earlier question: who could you make it with?

Notes & connections

2021–2023 / making a place to return

A handle became a collaborator.

For Three Oscillators, joining that world meant getting on a weekly call at 2:30 a.m. in Mumbai. The time matters. Behind an online handle was a musician fitting unfamiliar people into the hours of an ordinary life.

Later, in CHAOS, he would make Walls with Boy Untitled and Lackhoney. Their account moves from calls and demos to decisions about vocals, words and production. The tools helped people find each other. Familiarity, attention and musical judgment turned that encounter into a song.

Notes & connections

February 2023 / the work between releases

A scene needed people who kept showing up.

Those rooms mattered beyond a launch. By February 2023, Future Surf Radio’s weekly schedule moved between rollout discussions, music reviews and technical conversations. Cam Murdoch appeared with different collaborators; Charm Taylor and Diamond Doll used a recurring session to build For The People DAO in public.

The newsletter sent listeners toward new releases too. A scene was taking shape through repetition: bring an idea, hear someone else’s work, learn something and return. Alongside that everyday work, artists were testing how a release could invite people in.

September 2021–2022 / artists try different paths

A record needed to happen.

Rewind to September 2021, when Daniel Allan asked listeners to help a record happen before it was finished. Daniel Allan’s Overstimulated campaign began in September 2021; the EP arrived the following January. People supported a record before they could hear the finished thing.

Allan connected his releases with a project treasury and promised it a portion of his artist share of master royalties. The arrangement was specific to this project. Its broader challenge was clear: could support arrive at the moment an artist needed to make the work, rather than only after it was complete?

Notes & connections

September 2021–2022 / artists try different paths

One song. More than one way to collect.

Sound’s first drop, OSHI’s CHILDHOOD, arrived in November 2021. Where Catalog foregrounded one-of-one records, Sound offered editions that several listeners could collect. The difference shaped the invitation: one song could have a group of identifiable supporters around it. Glass brought another kind of work into view, treating video as the release itself.

Musicians, filmmakers and performers were testing different forms of participation. The question was no longer simply whether a song could be collected. It was what collecting could make possible around the work.

September 2021–2022 / artists try different paths

The format shaped the experiment.

Soulection+ Radio: UNTITLED 001 brought several artists into one listening experience. Sound introduced Song Splits alongside the project, using 0xSplits to route proceeds to multiple recipients. Here the experiment reached into the arrangement between collaborators.

A shared release needed a way to share what came back. That practical problem leads into one of the era’s most ambitious projects: a band large enough to include musicians, visual artists, developers and organizers, all building the release together.

2022 / making together

Strangers. A deadline. A shared song.

Songcamp’s CHAOS launch account described 77 contributors, including 45 musicians. Rotating trios wrote in acts; other participants built artwork, stories, software and radio around the songs. The release arrived in packs, with a digital festival on June 3, 2022.

Even the form of the band was an experiment. Rather than gather behind one frontperson, contributors built a structure that could keep producing new combinations. To understand what that scale meant musically, follow one trio into one recording.

Notes & connections

2022 / making together

Meet the trio.

Lackhoney sent unused vocal demos. Boy Untitled heard his own relationship in Walls, then reshaped its words and topline. Three Oscillators built the production around the vocal.

In their oral history, the artists describe files moving between time zones and work taking shape around demo calls. CHAOS had arranged the encounter; the song came from what each person heard in the others’ contributions. This is the collective at its most intimate: an unfinished idea becoming something none of the three brought into the room alone.

May 2023 / another collaboration

A credit leads somewhere else.

In May 2023, Three Oscillators appeared on Sound of Fractures’ NOKMLY remix release with AmbieNFT, Kabuki and Green Ring. Jamie Reddington, the artist behind Sound of Fractures, wanted different artist communities to meet through music. The shared credit takes us from a temporary band to another musician’s song and another collaborative task.

Listen across the two projects and the network becomes audible. It is made of people carrying their practice into new settings, rather than a fixed roster belonging to one platform.

2022 / meeting in person

The people behind the handles took the stage.

LATASHÁ helped make places where the online scene could meet in person. Zoratopia’s Austin program billed Black Dave in March 2022. In June, reporting from New York described three days of programming, with performances by LATASHÁ and Iman Europe.

The work people had encountered through profiles and release pages could now be heard together. These gatherings opened another part of the story: what happened when the people behind the handles shared a room—and what they made after they left it.

Afterward / an unfinished history

A token can outlast a website.

The promise of a record that could move beyond one storefront eventually met a harder test: the storefront closing. Sound announced its website would go offline in January 2026 as its team focused on Vault. Catalog’s closing account described successive attempts to keep its model working.

A token, a recording and the place people discover it have different lives. The token’s history may survive while a listening experience disappears. Independence from a platform still leaves work to do: finding the music, keeping it accessible and remembering why it mattered.

Afterward / an unfinished history

Someone still has to keep the door open.

The Park’s own archive gathers releases scattered across Catalog, Sound and Zora. A listener can follow a song to its credits, a playlist or an original edition without remembering which storefront first hosted it. That is practical work, and it changes what remains available to discover.

This documentary tries a smaller version of the same task: follow the recording back to the people and the media it names. A record of ownership can survive. Keeping the music findable still takes care.

Pete’s perspective / shared for this documentary

What did people carry forward?

For Pete, the lasting change was where he located value: in people, their work and their ability to make things together. He connects that experience to becoming a technology entrepreneur. With new software tools, he sees less reason to wait for a platform to build what his community needs.

His perspective brings us back to the question we began with. Who gets to decide what happens around a song? This chapter follows artists trying to take a larger part in that answer—by making records, making room for one another, and sometimes making the tools themselves.
